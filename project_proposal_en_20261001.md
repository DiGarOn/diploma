# Characteristics of a Block-Cipher Key-Recovery Method Using Probabilistic Linear Relations

## Abstract

This study experimentally examines assumptions underlying linear cryptanalysis by relating the amount of available plaintext-ciphertext material to the recovery of two final-round keys. The model is a 12-round educational Feistel cipher with a 16-bit block and a 32-bit master key. For each experimental key, the linear relation over the first ten rounds with the greatest absolute imbalance is selected. Ciphertexts are partially decrypted under every possible pair of final-round keys, and the empirical imbalance of the selected relation is evaluated for each hypothesis. Three adaptive material sizes are proportional to the inverse square of the selected imbalance; a fourth uses the complete plaintext space. Four series of 524,288 experiments were completed for the original cipher and four for a variant with XOR round-key injection. In both variants, more material is associated with a higher frequency of true-key recovery and a larger mean ratio of true to false absolute imbalances. On complete material, the true pair ranked first in 94.73% of experiments for the original cipher and 98.53% for the XOR variant. The results characterize the studied cipher and independent experimental series; they do not establish that changing the key-injection operation alone caused the difference between variants.

**Keywords:** linear cryptanalysis, block cipher, linear probabilistic relation, round-key recovery, linear imbalance.

## Introduction

Linear cryptanalysis uses relations among selected input, output, and key bits whose probability of holding differs from one half [1], [2]. If the resulting imbalance is sufficiently pronounced, observations from multiple plaintext-ciphertext pairs can support a ranking of candidate round keys. However, the existence of a detectable linear relation does not guarantee that the correct candidate will lead that ranking for a finite sample. The empirical imbalance varies with the selected texts, while the largest score among many false candidates may be unusually high. Consequently, a statement about the strongest relation alone cannot describe the practical reliability of key recovery.

The present study addresses this gap in a small, fully enumerable cipher. Its 16-bit block makes it possible to compare restricted samples with all $2^{16}$ plaintexts. The 32-bit master key and 12-round Feistel structure are specified independently [3]; the experimental method selects a relation over the first ten rounds and tests hypotheses for the two final-round keys by partial decryption [4]. This is an experimental model, not a security assessment of a standardized cipher. Its value lies in exposing the relationship between the amount of material, the measured imbalance under true and false hypotheses, and the rank of the true pair.

The research problem therefore concerns both the effect of material size on the rank of the true final-round key pair and the extent to which its empirical imbalance separates from the mean imbalance under false pairs. The objective is to quantify both effects in completed, independent series. The analysis selects a key-dependent linear relation, evaluates all candidate pairs on each selected sample, and compares aggregate rankings and imbalance statistics across four material regimes. A supplementary XOR version of the cipher provides a further set of observations, without being treated as a controlled, paired comparison.

## Related Work

Matsui's linear-cryptanalysis method connects a probabilistic linear relation to statistical hypotheses about secret-key material [1]. Nyberg's analysis of linear approximations provides a broader theoretical treatment of relations in block ciphers [2]. These works explain why a nonzero imbalance can carry information, but neither supplies a success rate for this particular educational cipher under its specific key schedule, sampling rule, and candidate-ranking procedure. Those quantities must be measured for the construction actually studied.

The implementation examined here makes that distinction explicit. The strongest absolute imbalance is determined for a ten-round prefix; then the last two round keys are guessed and the observed relation is scored after partial decryption [4]. The two steps answer different questions. A strong relation describes the prefix transformation under one true master key, whereas recovery requires that its empirical score distinguish the true final-round hypothesis from 65,535 alternatives. Earlier numerical summaries for the original cipher [5] and the XOR variant [6] provide the completed observations analyzed below. Their separate seeds make aggregate comparison possible but do not create paired outcomes for identical master keys.

## Methods

One experiment is associated with a sampled 32-bit master key. For that key, the first ten rounds define a 16-bit permutation. For nonzero input and output masks $\alpha$ and $\beta$, respectively, a linear relation compares the binary scalar products of $\alpha$ with the plaintext and $\beta$ with the ten-round output. Its signed imbalance is the difference between the fractions of inputs for which the relation equals zero and one. A fast Walsh-Hadamard transform is used to find the relation with the greatest absolute imbalance. Denote that maximum by $\delta$. Because the prefix depends on the master key, its selected masks and $\delta$ can change between experiments [4].

The experiment then constructs a set of plaintext-ciphertext pairs. In the three adaptive regimes, the number of distinct plaintexts is $N=\min\{2^{16},\lceil m/\delta^2\rceil\}$, where $m$ equals 1, 5, or 10. Plaintexts are sampled without replacement. The fourth regime contains every plaintext in the 16-bit domain. For each key, that regime can also be regarded as choosing a sufficiently large coefficient to reach the sample cap; a single universal fourth value is not asserted. Distinct random seeds were used for the four series of each cipher variant. Each series comprises $2^{19}=524{,}288$ experiments [4]–[6].

Every selected plaintext is encrypted under the true master key. Its ciphertext is then partially decrypted for every possible pair of the final two 8-bit round keys. There are $2^{16}$ candidate pairs. The selected ten-round relation is evaluated between the original plaintext and the resulting intermediate state. For each candidate, the absolute value of its empirical imbalance is the ranking score. The true pair's rank is recorded, together with whether it appears in the top one, three, or 32 candidates. The phrase *key recovery* in this article refers specifically to the tested pair of round keys; the experiment does not establish recovery of the complete 32-bit master key [4].

Two further statistics distinguish a typical false hypothesis from the best false hypothesis. Within each experiment, let $D_{\mathrm{true}}$ be the absolute empirical imbalance of the true pair, and let $\overline{D}_{\mathrm{false}}$ be the mean absolute empirical imbalance of the 65,535 false pairs. The reported ratio is the mean across experiments of $D_{\mathrm{true}}/\overline{D}_{\mathrm{false}}$, **not** a ratio formed after taking separate series-level means. The maximum false score is considered separately because a large mean ratio does not imply that the true pair outranks every false pair. A supplementary last-four-round product of absolute imbalances was also retained, but it is analyzed separately from the primary ranking outcome [4]–[6].

The original cipher and the XOR key-injection variant were evaluated using the same four material regimes. The latter replaces addition modulo $2^8$ by XOR inside the round function [3], [4]. No additional runs are planned. Interpretation is restricted to the saved aggregate results: independent key sets and different realized sample counts prevent a causal claim based solely on the difference between variants. Likewise, the full-material regime is a property of the small 16-bit model rather than a feasible prescription for large-block production ciphers.

## Results

In the original cipher, mean sample sizes were approximately 1,123, 5,615, and 11,225 plaintexts for $m=1$, $m=5$, and $m=10$, followed by 65,536 in the complete-material regime. The true round-key pair ranked first in 0.45%, 3.85%, 14.60%, and 94.73% of the respective 524,288-experiment series [5]. Its mean rank declined from approximately 23,103 to 6,655, 1,378, and 1.75. The progression establishes a clear descriptive association between more material and more successful identification of the true pair in these completed series. It does not show perfect recovery: 27,646 full-material experiments still lacked a first-place true pair.

The mean within-experiment ratio $D_{\mathrm{true}}/\overline{D}_{\mathrm{false}}$ increased from 1.4465 to 2.7370, 3.7801, and 8.0392 across the same four regimes [5]. This rise directly supports the hypothesis that the true relation becomes more distinct, on average, from false-key relations as material grows. Yet an average over all false hypotheses does not control their maximum. The true candidate may still lose to a particularly strong false candidate, which explains why statistical separation and a top-one recovery rate are related but not interchangeable measures. In the original-cipher data, the supplementary last-four-round statistic also failed to separate the true product from the maximum false product: the latter exceeded the former on average in all four regimes [5].

The XOR variant exhibited the same broad material-dependent pattern on independent key sets. Its mean selected plaintext counts were about 572, 2,856, 5,712, and 65,536. The top-one proportions were 1.0025%, 8.2823%, 25.1808%, and 98.5300%; mean true ranks declined from 22,770 to 6,975, 1,695, and 2.29 [6]. Even on complete material, the true pair was not first in 7,707 of 524,288 experiments. Accordingly, the higher top-one proportion for this variant must not be described as certain recovery.

The XOR series' mean within-experiment true/false ratio increased from 1.4384 to 2.6650, 3.6083, and 8.2255 [6]. Together with the original-cipher sequence, this is evidence for the proposed direction of statistical separation in both tested constructions. In contrast, the saved XOR last-four-round products for true, maximum-false, and mean-false candidates coincided, giving a ratio of one; this auxiliary statistic did not distinguish candidate pairs in those data [6]. The main result therefore rests on the key ranks and empirical imbalances, not on that tail measure.

Although the full-material top-one proportions differ between constructions, a direct operator effect cannot be isolated from these results. Their experimental master keys differ, as do the adaptive regimes' realized sample sizes. The reported percentages describe each completed set of independent trials. They support comparison of observed patterns, but not an assertion that the XOR substitution alone accounts for the numerical difference.

## Conclusion

This study links a selected linear relation, partial decryption under candidate final-round keys, and the observed success of round-key recovery as the material size changes. Across four completed regimes for each cipher variant, larger samples were associated with higher top-one recovery proportions and larger mean within-experiment ratios of true to false absolute imbalance. The ratio's increase is the central evidence for the hypothesized statistical separation. At the same time, strong false candidates remain possible, and complete material does not produce a 100% top-one rate. The supplementary last-four-round statistic did not independently establish recovery.

The findings are confined to the educational cipher and the saved independent experimental series. No further experiments are proposed; the remaining work consists of checking, presenting, and interpreting the existing results. In particular, any claim about the isolated effect of XOR key injection would require a comparison design not supplied by these runs.
